How we read this brief

Strategy, positioning and tone of voice are already defined on your side — we leave them untouched and treat them as input. This engagement is not a rebrand. It takes what already exists (logo, product design system, marketing angles) and builds the missing layer above it: a graphic language, and the rules that let anyone on the team apply it without asking a designer.

Derive the graphic language

One approved direction

Prove it on real surfaces

Execution roadmap

Three weeks, five stages. The direction is locked at the end of week one — everything after that is application, not exploration.

01
Asset Audit & System Map
Days 1–3

This stage establishes what the brand already has before anything new is drawn. We take the existing logo, product design system, marketing angles, brand vision and tone-of-voice guidelines and read them as one system, identifying which elements are stable, which need to be expanded for marketing use, and which do not exist yet. The output is the shared factual base the rest of the engagement builds on.

This stage involves
  • Asset inventory—Structured review of every existing brand and product asset provided as input.
  • System map—Shows how product design tokens and brand elements currently relate to each other.
  • Gap list—Names what marketing surfaces need that the current system does not yet define.
  • Kickoff alignment—One call to confirm interpretation, priorities and the final list of applied formats.
02
Graphic Language Concepts
Days 4–7

This stage produces the creative decision the whole engagement depends on. We develop three distinct directions for the graphic language — pattern, shape, composition and imagery treatment — each derived from the existing identity rather than placed beside it. Every direction is shown on the same two sample surfaces so the choice is made on evidence, and one direction is approved before any rollout begins.

This stage involves
  • Three directions—Distinct takes on pattern, shape language and composition, each with a stated rationale.
  • Sample surfaces—Each direction shown on two identical surfaces for a like-for-like comparison.
  • Selection round—Review session where one direction is approved, with one round of refinement.
  • Direction lock—Written confirmation of the approved direction, closing exploration for this engagement.
03
Core Identity Definition
Days 8–12

This stage turns the approved direction into rules. It covers how the existing mark may and may not be used, how typography behaves in marketing contexts as opposed to product UI, how the palette expands beyond the product tokens, and what the pattern and icon set contains. These are the pages a new designer, agency or contractor reads first.

This stage involves
  • Logo usage guidelines—Clear space, minimum sizes, permitted variants, backgrounds and explicit misuse cases.
  • Marketing typography—Display and body scale for marketing surfaces, mapped against the product type system.
  • Expanded colour palette—Marketing-level palette with usage ratios, pairings and accessibility contrast notes.
  • Imagery direction—Defines subject, treatment and crop rules, with a reference board for sourcing.
  • Pattern & icon library—Small reusable set built from the approved graphic language, ready to extend later.
04
Applied Examples Rollout
Days 13–18

This stage proves the system holds across formats. The approved language is applied to each requested surface at the level needed to demonstrate the rules — layout logic, hierarchy and element usage — rather than as final production artwork. The exact list of print, merch and social formats is confirmed at kickoff and sized to fit within this stage.

This stage involves
  • Social media—Post templates showing how the language holds in feed formats and profile assets.
  • LinkedIn set—Account design plus one carousel example demonstrating narrative pacing across slides.
  • Email—Marketing email layout with header, content blocks and signature treatment.
  • Ad creative—Creative set in the agreed channel sizes, showing message and visual hierarchy.
  • Print & merch—Selected items from banners, stickers, business cards, flyers, posters and merch.
  • Presentation slides—Example slide layouts covering title, content, data and section-break cases.
  • Website mockup—One light page mockup indicating direction, not a build-ready design.
05
Brand Book Assembly & Handoff
Days 19–21

This stage delivers the system in a form the team can use without us. Rules and applied examples are compiled into one document, source files are organised and handed over, and a walkthrough covers how to apply the language to surfaces that were not part of this scope.

This stage involves
  • Compiled brand book—Single document covering core identity rules and every applied example produced.
  • Source files—Editable Figma file structured so the team can extend the system themselves.
  • Exported assets—Logo variants, patterns and icons exported in the formats each team needs.
  • Handoff walkthrough—One session covering how to apply the rules to future surfaces and edge cases.

To confirm before we start

The package is fixed at $3 000 — the answers below size the applied examples stage so the right formats get covered. Nothing here changes the price; it changes where the effort lands.

01

Print & merch — which items actually matter? The brief lists banners, stickers, business cards, flyers and posters. We would rather do five items properly than eleven superficially. Which ones do you print or order most often, and in what formats and sizes?

02

Merch — what physical items are planned? Apparel, tote, notebook, drinkware, conference giveaways? Each behaves differently under a pattern system, so the list shapes how the graphic language is built.

03

Social — which platforms beyond LinkedIn? Instagram, X, Facebook, YouTube? And roughly how many recurring post types do you actually run, so templates map to real content rather than hypothetical ones.

04

Email — marketing or transactional? A newsletter or campaign layout, a transactional template, or a signature and header set. These are different deliverables.

05

Ad creative — which channels and sizes? LinkedIn, Meta, Google Display and programmatic all have different constraints. Naming the priority channel keeps the creative set usable.

06

Presentations — which deck types? Sales, investor, conference or internal. And which tool the team builds in, since Figma, Google Slides and PowerPoint each constrain what a template can do.

07

Website mockup — which page? Homepage is the usual answer. Desktop only, or desktop plus one mobile view?

08

Product design system — what access do we get? Read-only reference, or may we extend its tokens so the brand and product layers stay in sync?

09

Approvals — who signs off, and how fast? The three-week timeline assumes one decision-maker and feedback within two business days at each review point.

10

Final format — how does the team want to consume this? PDF brand book, a living Figma file, or both. We default to both.
